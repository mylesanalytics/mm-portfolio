PART 01 / 05THE INVISIBLE ASSIST

The game of basketball is not what you think it is. It's not what the box score tells you, nor is it what those highlight reels we've become so obsessed with show us. To me, it's 48 minutes of beautiful "chaotic synchrony." I say beautiful because the dopamine rush, mixed with our natural competitive spirit, makes experiencing this game unlike anything else. Yet, in the midst of all that chaos, basketball remains the ultimate team sport, and I believe anyone who doesn't view it that way is missing something fundamental about the game.

Maybe it's all the one-on-ones we've grown accustomed to seeing and/or being apart of, that have our minds so fixated on the individual player. Whatever the reason, I think we've become too focused on individual performances and not nearly focused enough on how those performances influence everything happening around them.

I want to detail my philosophy on the game of basketball and explain what's driving the work within my portfolio. My goal is to uncover the interconnected pieces of the game that unfold on the court and, more importantly, find ways to quantify the actual value each player contributes to their team's success.

Let me give you an example.

An All-Star player like Luka Dončić gets pressured while bringing the ball up the court following an inbound pass. Once he crosses half-court, he's met with a double-team. Now, naturally, this creates an opportunity for someone else on his team to get open. With Luka's size, court vision, and height advantage over most guards defending him, he's capable of making an effective pass to that open teammate.

That player now has a greater opportunity to make a high-percentage play, whether it's an open shot, a drive to the basket, or another pass that leads to an even better scoring opportunity. Essentially, Luka's presence and the defensive attention he commands have created a situation in which his teammates can operate more efficiently.

Now, we all understand that when a player draws a double-team, it can create an advantage for the offense and a disadvantage for the defense. But here's my question: How much of an advantage? And how much of a disadvantage?

Well, that depends on several variables.

It depends on the player being doubled, the two defenders applying the pressure, the teammate who's been freed up, and the help defender responsible for rotating over to cover that open player. But what happens when that rotation creates another mismatch? Now another defender has to help, which creates another opportunity somewhere else on the floor, and so on and so forth.

One decision creates another, which creates another — and the box score might never notice.

One decision creates another, which creates another, and suddenly the entire possession has been influenced by an action that might never appear in the box score.

These are the situations that traditional statistics don't fully capture.

And because of that, when people look at box scores or listen to media discussions built primarily around individual statistics, I believe it can lead to misguided narratives and a misunderstanding of how teams actually win basketball games.

PART 02 / 05DEFENSE AS INFLUENCE

Now, let's look at the defensive side of the ball, because I believe this is where things become even more interesting.

How do we quantify the actual value of a player's defensive contributions, particularly when that player is part of a highly efficient defensive team?

Let's take Draymond Green, for example.

Green is known for his ability to defend multiple positions, recognize offensive actions, communicate defensive assignments, and provide help defense. But how much do his teammates benefit from his presence? And to what extent is their individual defensive effectiveness being enhanced simply because Green is on the floor?

Let's say Steph Curry is guarding Shai Gilgeous-Alexander. SGA is surveying the floor, trying to determine his next move. But here's the interesting part: SGA's decision might not be based entirely on Steph, the player directly guarding him. Instead, he might be paying just as much attention to where Draymond Green is positioned.

Does he attack Steph off the dribble? Does he try to draw Green into helping, potentially creating an opportunity for a teammate? Or does Green's positioning discourage that drive altogether, forcing SGA into an entirely different decision?

Let's say SGA attempts to initiate an action that draws Green's attention, but the defense rotates effectively. Steph recognizes the developing play, anticipates the passing lane, and intercepts a bad pass.

Steph gets credited with the steal.

If that steal leads to a fast break and an easy basket, the possession contributes positively to the team's scoring margin while both Steph and Draymond are on the floor. Their plus-minus numbers reflect that change in score, but they don't tell us how much each player actually contributed to creating the turnover.

And what about Draymond?

He didn't record the steal. He didn't block a shot. He might not have even touched the ball. Yet his positioning, defensive awareness, and influence on SGA's decision-making may have played a significant role in how that entire possession unfolded.

Sure, lineup-based defensive ratings and other impact metrics might capture some of that value over time. But they don't necessarily tell us how much Green's presence influenced that particular play or how much easier he made Steph's defensive assignment.

So where does that value show up?

That's what I'm interested in figuring out.

I understand that quantifying this level of influence is incredibly difficult. But I believe it's possible to develop better ways of measuring how a player's offensive and defensive effectiveness influences the performance of the teammates around them.

And I believe this type of analysis can lead to a much more accurate understanding of a player's true value.

It would allow us to go beyond simply asking what a player produces individually and start asking how much better, or worse, the team performs because of that player's presence and actions.

Of course, watching film remains essential. But imagine being able to identify and quantify many of these contributions through data without having to spend countless hours manually reviewing every possession just to recognize patterns that traditional statistics overlook.

That could fundamentally change how we evaluate players.

Because when you're building a basketball team, it's not just about collecting talent. It's about fit. It's about finding the right players for the system, the style of basketball you're trying to play, and, most importantly, the players already on your roster.

PART 03 / 05THE ECONOMICS OF FIT

And that brings me to another important component of my philosophy: the relationship between player production and monetary value.

In other words, what are you actually getting for what you're paying?

Just because a player isn't lighting up the box score doesn't necessarily mean he's failing to live up to his contract. And the opposite is equally true. A player putting up impressive individual numbers isn't automatically providing value proportional to what his team is paying him.

This is where I think about the old Moneyball concept of identifying undervalued and overvalued players.

But I want to take that idea a little further.

I'm not necessarily interested in identifying players who are universally undervalued or overvalued across the league. I'm interested in identifying players who are undervalued or overvalued relative to a specific team's system, roster construction, and style of basketball.

A player who produces relatively unimpressive numbers on one team could turn out to be an absolute steal for another team because his particular skill set complements what that team already has.

That's where I believe tremendous value can be found.

But how do you identify those players?

You can't simply look at the box score or compare their salaries and expect to understand that relationship. You have to evaluate the player's production, the context in which that production occurs, and the cost associated with acquiring that production.

And the production itself needs to be measured more comprehensively.

Christopher E. Baker and Stephen M. Shea, in their book Basketball Analytics, discuss what they call bottom-up and top-down approaches to measuring player value. These approaches provide different perspectives on a player's contribution, whether we're examining individual actions and events or evaluating how those contributions translate into broader team outcomes.

I believe combining these perspectives is essential to developing more complete player evaluations.

Now, take those evaluations and connect them to a team's salary cap, existing contracts, financial flexibility, and the restrictions associated with acquiring players through trades and free agency.

That's when things start getting really interesting.

PART 04 / 05PREDICTING CONTRIBUTION

And this leads directly into another area I want to explore: predicting how successful a particular player will be within a specific team.

Here's an example.

Let's say there's a player on the market who's an efficient three-point shooter and an excellent on-ball defender. On paper, that sounds like an incredible asset for just about any basketball team.

But what if the team considering him is already one of the most efficient three-point shooting teams in the league and desperately needs to improve defensively?

Naturally, they might target this player because he provides something they need defensively while also complementing something they already do well offensively.

Makes sense, right?

But let's say acquiring that player comes with a significant financial commitment. Maybe his contract pushes the team deeper into first-apron territory, closer to the second apron, where additional roster-building restrictions become increasingly consequential. Depending on how the acquisition is structured, the team might also face hard-cap limitations.

Now we're talking about a costly decision.

That player had better be worth it.

And maybe your analysis predicts that he will be. But how accurate is that prediction? More importantly, how does his projected impact compare to other players on the market who might be cheaper or even more expensive but provide a different combination of skills?

For example, what if another available player is also an efficient three-point shooter? He's not an elite on-ball defender, but he's above average defensively and a significantly better rebounder relative to his position.

Now you've got two players who both appear to make your team better, but in different ways.

One provides elite perimeter defense. The other gives you respectable defense, strong shooting, and a substantial advantage on the glass.

Which one actually improves your team's chances of winning more games?

And when you factor in their salaries, the team's existing roster, and the financial restrictions associated with acquiring either player, which one represents the better overall value?

That's the question I want to be able to answer with greater accuracy.

Because the answer isn't necessarily the player with the better individual statistics, the more recognizable name, or even the better overall talent level.

It's the player whose specific contributions are projected to generate the greatest improvement in the team's performance relative to the resources required to acquire him.

That's a completely different way of looking at player value.

My goal is to develop metrics and predictive models that can help answer these questions. But doing that effectively requires the more advanced player-impact measurements I discussed earlier.

If we can better understand how individual players influence the actions, decisions, and effectiveness of their teammates, then we can begin building models that predict how those same players might perform in entirely different team environments.

A player's contribution is not a fixed property. Context changes everything.

Of course, those predictions will never be perfect. Basketball has far too many variables, and player performance is influenced by more than just measurable skill sets. Coaching, chemistry, roles, injuries, matchups, and even the decisions players make from possession to possession can change everything.

But that's precisely why I believe there's so much opportunity in this type of analysis.

Ideally, this would involve proprietary, team-specific analytical models designed around an organization's roster, playing style, and competitive objectives. Attempting to develop that same level of detailed analysis for every team in the league would be an enormous undertaking.

However, with advancements in technology, particularly spatial tracking, player-tracking systems, and the growing ability to collect more detailed bottom-up measurements, I believe we're getting closer to making this type of work more practical and scalable.

We can now capture aspects of the game that were once incredibly difficult to measure. Player movement, defensive positioning, spacing, off-ball activity, and the interactions between players can all provide valuable information about what's actually happening on the floor.

But having access to more data is only part of the equation.

What matters just as much is how we interpret that data, the questions we choose to ask, and the philosophy guiding our analysis.

PART 05 / 05WHY THIS MATTERS

And ultimately, that's what this portfolio is about.

It's about how I see the game of basketball, how I understand team success, and how I want to use data science to challenge some of the conventional ways we evaluate player performance.

Basketball is an incredibly fluid game. Every action is connected to another, and every decision is influenced by a multitude of variables. The game is constantly evolving from one possession to the next, and understanding those relationships requires us to look beyond the obvious.

There's a tremendous amount to account for, and I recognize just how challenging it will be to measure all of it accurately.

But that's also what makes this work so fascinating to me.

I don't just want to know what happened on the basketball court. I want to understand why it happened, who actually influenced it, and how much their influence contributed to the outcome.

Because to me, that's where the true value of a basketball player begins to reveal itself.

And with attention to detail, focus, hard work, and dedication, greatness is destined!

R.I.P. to the legend, Kobe Bryant and God Bless.
